Exploring Income Inequality Across NYC Census Block Groups

An exploratory analysis of household income, demographics, and neighborhood characteristics across 5,852 census block groups in New York City.

Research overview

How does household income vary across New York City, and what can neighborhood-level demographic data reveal about urban inequality? This project uses a dataset of 5,852 census block groups and 42 variables to explore differences in income, population, age, race, housing density, and access-related characteristics.

Rather than relying only on citywide averages, the analysis examines smaller geographic units. This approach makes it possible to identify concentrations of disadvantage and generate more specific questions about the distribution of resources and opportunities across the city.

The dataset identifies its period as 2012–2016. Findings below describe this historical dataset, not present-day NYC income levels.

Selected findings

Income varies substantially across block groups

The mean block-group median household income is approximately $64,295, compared with a median of $57,428. The standard deviation is about $36,586. The income distribution is not normal, with a pronounced higher-income tail.

More than a third of block groups fall below $50,000

2,261 of 5,852 block groups have a median household income below $50,000.

Income and demographic characteristics can be examined together

For the 1,549 block groups where at least 40% of residents fall within the dataset's PctBlackOnly measure, the unweighted mean of block-group median household incomes is approximately $49,251. This is a descriptive subset statistic, not a causal finding or a measure of individual household incomes.

Older populations and lower incomes overlap in some areas

The notebook identifies 67 block groups where more than 30% of residents are over age 65 and median household income is below $40,000. This intersection may help frame further questions about aging, accessibility, and service provision.

High-income values extend into a long upper tail

A borough-colored income histogram in the notebook suggests that Manhattan accounts for much of the upper-income tail, with Brooklyn also represented. This is a qualitative reading of the plotted distribution and would benefit from further borough-level analysis.

Data and methods

The project reads an NYC census block-group CSV into a pandas DataFrame and uses descriptive statistics, conditional filtering, and exploratory visualization to examine income and demographic patterns.

  • Data structure: 5,852 rows, each representing a census block group, with 42 variables.
  • Python and pandas: Data loading, inspection, filtering, sums, and descriptive statistics.
  • Matplotlib and Seaborn: A 50-bin household-income histogram colored by borough and a scatterplot of income against housing density, differentiated by waterfront status.
  • Geographic context: Census block-group identifiers, coordinates, borough labels, and proximity indicators for parks are included in the source dataset.

Interpretation and limitations

This is an exploratory analysis of a historical dataset, not a comprehensive study of the causes of income inequality. Block-group medians should not be confused with individual or household incomes, and the mean of block-group medians is not the citywide median household income.

Likewise, the analysis identifies geographic and demographic associations but does not establish causal relationships. Although the dataset contains coordinates and other geographic indicators, the supplied notebook does not produce a geographic map; mapping and further statistical tests would be logical next steps.

The source CSV is referenced in the notebook by a local computer path. To make the project fully reproducible, the dataset would need to be supplied or replaced with a documented publicly available source.
